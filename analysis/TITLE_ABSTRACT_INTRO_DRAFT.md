# Title

**The Solopreneur Share Is Falling, Not Rising: Evidence from Self-Employed Software Developers, 2019–2024**

# Abstract

Digital technology is often credited with enabling a growing class of
self-employed workers — sometimes called solopreneurs — who work alone by
choice, earning through a product or platform rather than billed hours.
Whether this phenomenon is empirically distinct from broader
self-employment, and whether it is actually becoming more common, has not
been tested against secondary labor-market data. This study tested
whether digital solopreneurship shows a rising, income-advantaged
signature in macro and individual-level labor data. Building on the
necessity/opportunity distinction in self-employment research, a
country-year panel from the World Bank (2005–2024) and a repeated
cross-sectional survey of self-employed and freelance software developers
(2019–2024, *N* = 47,322) were combined with a three-criterion operational
definition of solopreneur status. Panel fixed-effects regression, logistic
trend models, and multiple imputation addressed country-year confounding,
sampling composition, and non-random missing income, respectively.
Internet penetration was weakly, negatively associated with aggregate
self-employment; the zero-employee share among self-employed developers
fell by more than a third between 2019 and 2024 rather than rising; and
zero-employee respondents earned approximately 21% less than otherwise
comparable peers who had built a team. These exploratory findings
challenge a straightforward growth narrative for digital solopreneurship
and are bounded by the non-probability, developer-specific survey sample.

# Introduction

More than 72.9 million Americans reported working independently in 2025,
part of a workforce researchers, platforms, and policymakers have tracked
for over a decade (MBO Partners, 2025). Within that broad category, a
narrower phenomenon has drawn increasing popular attention: the
"solopreneur," a self-employed individual who not only works alone but
does so by choice, builds a product or platform-based income stream rather
than selling hours, and has no stated intention of hiring. Whether this
narrower phenomenon is empirically distinguishable from self-employment in
general — and whether it is in fact becoming more common, as much of the
discourse around it assumes — is an open, testable empirical question that
this study addresses directly.

Academic accounts of self-employment offer a starting point but not a
settled answer. Labor economists have long distinguished necessity-driven
from opportunity-driven entry into self-employment (Amit & Muller, 1995;
Reynolds et al., 2001), and have documented that self-employment carries
an earnings penalty relative to employment on average (Hamilton, 2000),
one that is not uniform but tends to be larger for non-professional
own-account workers in higher-income countries specifically (Gindling et
al., 2016). At the aggregate level, self-employment rates are understood
to decline as economies formalize (International Labour Organization,
2025). None of this literature, however, isolates the zero-employee,
deliberately-non-scaling subgroup that the solopreneur label picks out, or
tests whether that subgroup's relative size has moved in a particular
direction over a defined recent period.

Industry and platform research has been more direct about growth claims,
and less consistent. MBO Partners' State of Independence series — the
longest-running research program on the U.S. independent workforce — has
tracked a broadly growing pool of independent workers across fifteen
consecutive annual reports (MBO Partners, 2025), a picture echoed in
platform-published research from Upwork and similar organizations. An
influential early academic estimate reached a similar conclusion before
its own authors revised it: Katz and Krueger (2019a) initially reported
that U.S. alternative work arrangements had risen from 10.7% to 15.8% of
the workforce between 2005 and 2015, only to find, using the government's
own Contingent Worker Supplement, that the share had in fact fallen to
10.1% by 2017 — a reversal traced to survey design rather than to a
genuine trend (Katz & Krueger, 2019b).

Independently of both academic and industry research, a visible community
of self-identified solopreneurs and "indie hackers" has grown on platforms
such as Reddit (e.g., r/solopreneur, r/indiehackers, r/SaaS) and dedicated
sites such as Indie Hackers, founded in 2016. This community-level
discourse — builders publicly sharing product launches, revenue figures,
and a stated preference for staying small — is itself part of what has
made "solopreneur" a term worth studying empirically, independently of
whether the underlying population it describes is actually growing.

Taken together, this leaves a specific gap that neither the academic
necessity/opportunity literature, the aggregate formalization literature,
industry workforce reports, nor community discourse resolves on its own:
none of them isolates a zero-employee, non-scaling self-employment
subgroup and tracks its relative size and earnings over a recent,
well-defined period using an explicit operational definition rather than
an umbrella category. Katz and Krueger's (2019a, 2019b) own reversal is
instructive here — it shows that survey-based growth claims about
non-standard work can be an artifact of measurement rather than a real
trend, a possibility that has not been checked for the solopreneur-specific
subgroup that industry reports and community discourse describe as
expanding.

This study therefore asks whether digital solopreneurship — operationalized
through a structural (zero-employee), intentional (no growth ambition),
and income-leverage (product/asset income rather than billed time)
definition, of which only the structural criterion is measurable in the
data used here — shows a rising, income-advantaged signature in macro
labor statistics and in individual developer career and income data. All
hypotheses tested below were formulated from this reasoning before the
outcome data were inspected, but their exact operational form was
finalized while working with the data; consistent with that history, they
are reported throughout as exploratory, hypothesis-generating findings
requiring independent replication, not as confirmatory tests of
pre-registered predictions. Resolving this question matters now
specifically because the 2019–2024 window spans a period of substantial
disruption in the technology labor market — including large-scale
layoffs beginning in 2022 — that plausibly affects the size and
composition of the self-employed developer population in ways not yet
documented.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. The Methods section
describes the three data sources, the operational definition of
solopreneur status, and the analytic strategy for each hypothesis,
including how missing data and multicollinearity were handled. The
Results section reports the macro panel model, the temporal trend in
solopreneur prevalence, and the income comparison, in that order. The
Discussion situates these findings against the academic and industry
literature introduced above, and the Conclusions address the study's
practical and theoretical implications, its methodological limitations,
and directions for future research.
