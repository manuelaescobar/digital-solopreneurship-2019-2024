This study asked whether digital solopreneurship — defined by a
zero-employee structural condition, an intention to remain small, and
income decoupled from hours worked — leaves a distinct empirical signature
in macro-level labor statistics and in individual-level career and income
data. The answer is mixed and, on one point, counter to the premise
motivating much of the popular discourse on this phenomenon (Katz &
Krueger, 2019a). At
the country level, internet penetration was associated with a small
*decline*, not a rise, in the aggregate self-employment rate (H1). Among
individual developers, the share reporting zero employees fell by more
than a third (from 52.0% to 32.1%) between 2019 and 2024 rather than
growing (H2), even though those who
remained zero-employee earned substantially less than their
similarly-experienced, similarly-located peers who had built a team (H3).
Taken together, these findings suggest that "digital solopreneurship" is
not currently visible as a rising share of the labor market in either the
data source examined here — it may instead be a stable or shrinking niche
within a self-employed population that itself remains heterogeneous.

## Convergence with prior work

The negative, if modest, macro-level association between internet
penetration and aggregate self-employment (H1) converges with the
well-established pattern in labor economics whereby self-employment rates
decline as economies formalize (International Labour Organization [ILO],
2025) — a process that tends to move in step with rising internet access
rather than independently of it. Because the World Bank's aggregate measure does
not distinguish subsistence or informal self-employment from
platform-enabled or knowledge-work self-employment, this result is
consistent with, rather than a challenge to, that literature: it indicates
that the dominant compositional force in the aggregate indicator is the
formalization effect, not the emergence of a distinct digital-solopreneur
segment large enough to reverse it.

The income gap documented in H3 — solopreneurs earning approximately 20.9%
less than other self-employed or freelance developers with comparable
experience — similarly aligns with two established findings in the
self-employment literature. First, the long-standing necessity/opportunity
distinction formalized by the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (Reynolds et
al., 2001), building on earlier push/pull accounts of entrepreneurial entry
(Amit & Muller, 1995), holds that a substantial share of own-account work
is undertaken by individuals without the option of scaling into an
employer role, not exclusively by those pursuing a deliberate lifestyle or
strategic choice to remain small. Second, self-employment has repeatedly been found to carry an earnings
penalty relative to employment on average, even after accounting for
selection into self-employment (Hamilton, 2000), and cross-country
evidence indicates this penalty for non-professional own-account workers
is not uniform but tends to be larger in higher-income countries (Gindling
et al., 2016) — a boundary condition worth noting given that the present
sample spans a wide mix of country income levels (see Sample Description),
including several of the most-represented countries at markedly different
income levels. The present data cannot distinguish which mechanism dominates
within the "Just me" subgroup, but the direction and size of the gap are
consistent with this established heterogeneity within self-employment
rather than with a uniformly advantaged solopreneur population.

## Divergence from prior work

The clearest point of divergence concerns the declining share of
zero-employee developers documented in H2. Industry reports frequently
cited in discussions of this phenomenon — including MBO Partners' *State
of Independence* series and Upwork's *Freelance Forward* reports — have
described a broadly growing independent workforce over the past decade,
and an influential early academic estimate reached a similar conclusion:
Katz and Krueger (2019a), using a purpose-built 2015 survey module,
reported that the share of U.S. workers in alternative work arrangements
had risen from 10.7% in 2005 to as high as 15.8% in 2015. Notably, the same
authors subsequently revisited this conclusion using the Bureau of Labor
Statistics' own Contingent Worker Supplement and found that the share had
in fact *fallen* to 10.1% by 2017, tracing the original, much larger
estimate to differences in survey design and question wording rather than
to a genuine trend (Katz & Krueger, 2019b). The present finding, specific
to professional software developers responding to an annual technical
survey between 2019 and 2024, parallels this later, corrected picture more
than the original growth narrative: rather than a rising zero-employee
share, the data show a decline of more than a third, with the steepest
single-year drop occurring between 2021 and 2022. A plausible source of
this divergence from the original growth narrative is sample- and
period-specific: broader "independent workforce" reports typically span
many occupations, while the present sample is restricted to a single,
comparatively high-skill occupational group during a period of substantial
disruption in the technology labor market, including layoffs affecting an
estimated 666,000 tech employees across roughly 3,500 companies between
2022 and 2024, concentrated in 2023 (Harvard Business School, 2023).
Whether laid-off or cautious developers responded by joining small teams
or co-founded ventures rather than remaining solo, or whether the survey's
respondent pool itself shifted in ways not captured by the country-level
control applied here, cannot be adjudicated with the repeated
cross-sectional design used in this study — a limitation directly
analogous to the survey-design sensitivity that Katz and Krueger (2019b)
identified in their own reversal.

# Conclusions

This study examined whether digital solopreneurship — operationalized
through a zero-employee structural criterion, sitting alongside the
intentional and income-leverage criteria that a single secondary dataset
cannot fully capture — shows a rising, self-employment-income-advantaged
signature in either macro labor statistics or individual developer data.
The answer, across three exploratory analyses, is no on both counts within
the specific data sources examined: the zero-employee share among
self-employed developers fell by more than a third between 2019 and 2024
rather than rising, this decline was not attributable to shifts in the countries
represented in the sample, and developers in this subgroup earned
approximately one-fifth less than otherwise-comparable self-employed peers
who had gone on to build a team.

The main contribution of this study is empirical and methodological rather
than purely theoretical: it triangulates a phenomenon — digital
solopreneurship — that no single official statistic measures directly,
combining a country-year macro indicator, a repeated cross-sectional
individual-level survey, and an explicit three-criterion operational
definition designed to make the construct's boundaries auditable rather
than implicit. In doing so, it documents a specific, falsifiable, and
counter-narrative empirical pattern — a declining rather than rising
zero-employee share among self-employed developers — that existing
industry reporting on the "rise of independent work" does not, by
construction, disaggregate finely enough to detect, and that even the
academic literature initially underestimated for related reasons of survey
design before self-correcting (Katz & Krueger, 2019a, 2019b).

Practically, the income gap documented here suggests that policy and
platform design aimed at supporting self-employed digital workers should
not treat "solopreneur" and "self-employed with a team" as
interchangeable: the zero-employee subgroup earned reliably less at the
same experience level, which is relevant to how benefits, taxation
thresholds, or platform fee structures calibrated to self-employment
status broadly are likely to under- or over-serve this specific subgroup,
particularly given that earnings penalties for own-account workers are not
uniform across contexts (Gindling et al., 2016). For individuals
considering remaining solo versus building a
team, the magnitude of the gap (~21%) is large enough to be a material
input to that decision, independent of any non-pecuniary preference for
staying small.

Theoretically, the findings complicate a straightforward "rise of the
solopreneur" account of digital work by showing that, at least in this
occupational population and time window, the zero-employee segment's
relative size moved in the opposite direction while remaining a
lower-earning position within self-employment. This is consistent with
composition-based explanations — in which digital tools lower the
formation cost of small teams as readily as they lower the cost of working
alone — rather than with an account in which digital leverage
straightforwardly favors staying solo over growing a team. This reading is
consistent with recent extensions of transaction-cost theory of the firm
to digital markets, which argue that lower coordination costs can shift
firm boundaries in either direction rather than uniformly favoring smaller
units (Nagle et al., 2025).

These conclusions are bounded by specific methodological limitations, not
generic ones. First, the Stack Overflow Developer Survey is a
convenience sample of respondents to a single platform's annual survey,
skewed toward developers already engaged with that specific community;
the declining trend documented in H2 describes this population, not
self-employment or solopreneurship in the labor market at large, and the
boundary condition under which it would fail to generalize is any
population without this platform's specific selection dynamics. Second,
the survey is a repeated cross-section, not a panel: no individual was
observed transitioning into or out of solopreneur status, so the year
effect in H2 is a description of population composition over time, and a
compositional shift on some unmeasured respondent characteristic — beyond
the country composition already ruled out — cannot be excluded as an
alternative to a genuine behavioral shift. Third, the macro-level finding
(H1) is ecological: country-year associations cannot support any claim
about individual behavior, and the null-to-negative result should not be
read as evidence against individual-level digital solopreneurship, only as
a limitation of what an aggregate indicator can detect. Fourth, the
solopreneur construct tested here captures only the structural
(zero-employee) criterion of the three-part operational definition
motivating this project; the intentional (no growth ambition) and
income-leverage (product/asset income versus hourly income) criteria could
not be measured from this survey, so "solopreneur" in this study's H2/H3
findings should be read narrowly as "zero-employee self-employed
developer," a necessary but not sufficient condition for the fuller
construct.

Future research should prioritize a genuine panel design that follows the
same individuals across the 2021–2022 window in which the sharp decline
documented here occurred, to distinguish a true behavioral transition (for
example, laid-off developers co-founding ventures) from sample
composition change — directly addressing the limitation above that a
repeated cross-section cannot resolve. A dedicated survey instrument
operationalizing all three criteria of the solopreneur construct
(structural, intentional, and income-leverage), distributed across
solopreneur-specific communities rather than a general developer survey,
would allow the fuller construct — rather than its structural proxy alone
— to be tested directly. Finally, replicating the income-gap analysis (H3)
in non-developer occupational populations would establish whether the
solopreneur income penalty documented here is specific to software
development or reflects a broader feature of zero-employee
self-employment.
